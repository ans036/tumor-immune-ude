\begin{abstract}
We present a study of Universal Differential Equations (UDEs) for modeling tumor--immune dynamics---a progressive modeling workflow in Julia's SciML ecosystem. We develop and compare two UDE architectures: (A) a dual-network hybrid approach that augments mechanistic Gompertz growth with neural networks to capture unmodeled immune dynamics ($R^2 = 0.897$), and (B) a structured-parameter UDE that uses neural networks to learn context-dependent parameters within classical Gompertz and Michaelis--Menten frameworks ($R^2 = 0.991$). Both variants enforce biological constraints through physics-informed loss functions. Cross-validation with validation-based early stopping yields mean $R^2 = 0.945$ on held-out groups. Our implementation leverages DifferentialEquations.jl, Flux.jl, and SciMLSensitivity.jl. Code and data are publicly available at \url{https://github.com/ans036/tumor-immune-ude}.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Universal Differential Equations; Scientific Machine Learning; Tumor--Immune Dynamics; Julia; SciML; Gompertz Growth; Michaelis--Menten Kinetics; Cross-Validation.

\section{Introduction}

Modeling tumor--immune interactions is a long-standing challenge in computational biology \cite{kuznetsov1994,depillis2005validated,vaghi2020gompertz}. Traditional mechanistic models offer interpretability but often underfit complex dynamics, while pure machine learning approaches lack biological constraints. Universal Differential Equations (UDEs) combine mechanistic structure with neural network flexibility, embedding trainable components within differential equation frameworks to learn missing dynamics while preserving known physics \cite{rackauckas2020ude,karniadakis2021physics}.

Julia's SciML ecosystem provides strong infrastructure for UDE development through DifferentialEquations.jl \cite{rackauckas2017de}, Flux.jl \cite{innes2018flux}, and SciMLSensitivity.jl for adjoint-based gradient computation \cite{bezanson2017julia,innes2019differentiable,rackauckas2019diffeqflux}. While UDEs have been applied in hydrology and epidemiology \cite{shen2023differentiable,aboelyazeed2023differentiable,dandekar2020machine}, their application to tumor--immune modeling with rigorous cross-validation remains underexplored.

This paper presents a progressive modeling workflow for tumor--immune dynamics in Julia's SciML ecosystem, with the following contributions: (i) a dual-network hybrid UDE that augments Gompertz growth with learned immune dynamics, (ii) a structured-parameter UDE where neural networks learn context-dependent parameters within classical frameworks, (iii) cross-validation with validation-based early stopping for robust evaluation, and (iv) a fully reproducible Julia implementation.

\section{Statement of need}
\label{sec:statement-of-need}

Researchers modeling tumor--immune interactions need tools that balance mechanistic interpretability with predictive accuracy. Existing approaches are either purely mechanistic models that underfit or black-box methods that lack biological grounding. Our Julia package provides a UDE-based framework that integrates domain knowledge through physics-informed constraints, learns interpretable biological parameters, and provides cross-validated forecasting---all within Julia's SciML ecosystem.

\section{State of the field}
\label{sec:state-of-the-field}

Classical tumor--immune models such as the Kuznetsov--Taylor model \cite{kuznetsov1994} and the de Pillis--Radunskaya system \cite{depillis2005validated} provide interpretable frameworks but are parameter-heavy and difficult to calibrate with sparse data. The Gompertz model \cite{vaghi2020gompertz} describes tumor growth kinetics well but lacks immune interaction terms. Neural ODEs \cite{chen2018node} and Physics-Informed Neural Networks \cite{raissi2019pinn} have shown promise in scientific applications. UDEs \cite{rackauckas2020ude} enable hybrid mechanistic-neural models, though existing applications in biology \cite{shen2023differentiable,aboelyazeed2023differentiable,dandekar2020machine} have not specifically targeted tumor--immune dynamics with cross-validated evaluation. Our work applies UDEs to this domain with biologically-constrained Gompertz growth and Michaelis--Menten immune killing, using a progressive workflow from hybrid to structured-parameter models.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Universal Differential Equations}

A UDE decomposes the dynamics of a system $\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt} = f(\mathbf{u}, t, \boldsymbol{\theta})$ as:
\begin{equation}
f(\mathbf{u}, t, \boldsymbol{\theta}) = f_{\text{known}}(\mathbf{u}, t, \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{mech}}) + f_{\text{unknown}}(\mathbf{u}, t, \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{nn}})
\label{eq:ude_decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $f_{\text{known}}$ encodes mechanistic knowledge and $f_{\text{unknown}}$ captures unmodeled dynamics through neural networks \cite{lu2021deeponet,massaroli2020dissecting,finlay2020train}. Gradients flow through both components via differentiable programming, enabling end-to-end optimization.

\subsection{Biological Foundation}

Tumor growth follows the Gompertz model:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dV}{dt} = rV\ln\left(\frac{K}{V}\right)
\label{eq:gompertz}
\end{equation}
where $V(t)$ is tumor volume, $r$ is the growth rate, and $K$ is the carrying capacity. Immune-mediated tumor suppression follows Michaelis--Menten kinetics:
\begin{equation}
R_{\text{kill}}(V, I) = \frac{c \cdot V \cdot I(t)}{h + V}
\label{eq:michaelis_menten_killing}
\end{equation}
where $R_{\text{kill}}$ denotes the immune killing rate, $I(t)$ is immune cell concentration, $c$ is the maximum killing rate constant (units: day$^{-1}$), and $h$ is the half-saturation constant (volume at which killing reaches half its maximum rate).

\subsection{Phase 1: Dual-Network Hybrid UDE}

The Gompertz model (Eq.~\ref{eq:gompertz}) describes tumor growth in isolation but does not account for immune cell interactions. Classical models like Kuznetsov--Taylor \cite{kuznetsov1994} address this through explicit effector-cell compartments, but require the modeler to prescribe the functional form of the immune interaction and to calibrate its biologically named parameters---several of which are difficult to identify from sparse data (we quantify this trade-off in Section~\ref{sec:param-count}). Our hybrid approach instead uses neural networks to \emph{learn} the immune interaction and residual temporal dynamics directly from data, while retaining the well-established Gompertz backbone. This avoids prescribing the functional form of immune suppression a priori, allowing the data to reveal which interactions matter. The hybrid UDE augments Gompertz growth with two neural networks:
\begin{align}
\frac{dV}{dt} &= \underbrace{r_g V \ln\left(\frac{K_g}{V}\right)}_{\text{Gompertz}} - \underbrace{\alpha_g \phi_{\text{IRN}}(V_n, I_n)}_{\text{Learned Immune}} + \underbrace{\beta w(V_n) \phi_{\text{TACN}}(V_n, t_n)}_{\text{Temporal Correction}}
\label{eq:dual_network_ude}
\end{align}
where $\phi_{\text{IRN}}: \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow [0,1]$ is an Immune Response Network (sigmoid output), $\phi_{\text{TACN}}: \mathbb{R}^2 \rightarrow [-1,1]$ is a Time-Aware Correction Network (tanh output), $w(V_n)$ is a sigmoid gate suppressing corrections at small volumes, and $r_g, K_g, \alpha_g, \beta$ are learnable parameters.

\subsection{Phase 2: Structured-Parameter UDE}

Phases~1 and~2 form a \emph{sequential} workflow: the hybrid model (Phase~1) serves as an exploratory tool whose learned network behaviors reveal that immune killing and temporal corrections are the dominant missing dynamics. Phase~2 then incorporates these insights into a more constrained architecture. Specifically, the structured-parameter UDE preserves the classical Gompertz + Michaelis--Menten equation structure but uses neural networks to make all parameters context-dependent:
\begin{align}
\frac{dV}{dt} &= \underbrace{r(V,t,I) \cdot V \ln\left(\frac{K(V,t,I)}{V}\right)}_{\text{Dynamic Gompertz}} - \underbrace{\frac{c(V,t,I) \cdot V \cdot I(t)}{h(V,t,I) + V}}_{\text{Dynamic Michaelis--Menten}}
\label{eq:structured_param_ude}
\end{align}
where neural networks learn the mappings:
\begin{align}
(r, K) &= \text{NN}_{\text{growth}}(V_n, t_n, I_n), \quad
(c, h) = \text{NN}_{\text{immune}}(V_n, t_n, I_n)
\end{align}
with activation functions enforcing bounds: $r \in [r_{\min}, r_{\max}]$, $K \geq 1.1V$, $c \in [c_{\min}, c_{\max}]$, $h \in [h_{\min}, h_{\max}]$.

\textbf{Extrapolation note.} Because the neural networks in Eq.~\ref{eq:structured_param_ude} take normalized time $t_n$ as a direct input, learned parameter functions may not generalize when $t$ extends far beyond the training range. The sigmoid-bounded activations mitigate divergence by constraining outputs to biologically plausible intervals, but long-term forecasts should be interpreted as extrapolations with increasing uncertainty (see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\textbf{Terminology note.} We call this the ``structured-parameter'' UDE because, although all four biological parameters are learned by neural networks (making this a neural model), the outputs are channeled through the classical Gompertz and Michaelis--Menten equation structure. The neural networks do not directly specify the dynamics; rather, they parameterize a fixed mechanistic template. This preserves the interpretability of each parameter's biological role while allowing context-dependent variation.

\subsection{Physics-Informed Constraints}

We enforce biological priors through several loss terms. A consistency prior ensures immune activity does not increase tumor volume:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{consistency}} = \lambda_{\text{cons}} \sum_{g,j} \max\left(0, \hat{V}^{\text{immune}}_g(t_{g,j}) - \hat{V}^{\text{no-immune}}_g(t_{g,j}) + \varepsilon\right)^2
\label{eq:consistency_prior}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{V}^{\text{immune}}_g(t_{g,j})$ denotes the model-predicted tumor volume (hat indicates a predicted quantity) for group $g$ at time point $t_{g,j}$ with immune effects included, and $\hat{V}^{\text{no-immune}}_g(t_{g,j})$ is the corresponding counterfactual prediction with immune cell input set to zero. The subscript $g$ indexes over tumor groups and $j$ indexes over observation time points within each group. The margin $\varepsilon = 0.05$ ensures a minimum separation. This term penalizes any instance where the immune-present trajectory exceeds the immune-absent trajectory, enforcing the biological prior that immune activity should suppress---not enhance---tumor growth.

A physics prior aligns learned immune parameters with observed killing rates via rank correlation:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}} = \lambda_{\text{phys}}\left(1 - \rho_{\text{rank}}(I_{\text{static}}, \hat{c}_{\text{kill}})\right)^2
\label{eq:physics_prior}
\end{equation}
The Phase~1 (hybrid UDE) total loss combines volume fitting, derivative matching, a smoothness penalty, physics priors, negativity penalties, and $L_2$ regularization:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}^{(1)} &= w_{\text{vol}}\,\lambda_{\text{vol}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{volume}}
+ w_{\text{deriv}}\,\lambda_{\text{deriv}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{deriv}} \nonumber\\
&\quad + \lambda_{\text{smooth}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{smooth}}
+ \lambda_{\text{phys}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}} \nonumber\\
&\quad + \lambda_{\text{neg}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{negativity}} + \lambda_{\text{reg}} \mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}
\label{eq:total_loss}
\end{align}
where the individual terms are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item $\mathcal{L}_{\text{volume}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{g,j}(\hat{V}_g(t_{g,j}) - V^{\text{obs}}_g(t_{g,j}))^2$: mean squared error between predicted and observed tumor volumes ($N$ = total data points across groups);
  \item $\mathcal{L}_{\text{deriv}} = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{g,j}\bigl(\dot{\hat{V}}_g(t_{g,j}) - \dot{V}^{\text{obs}}_g(t_{g,j})\bigr)^2$: derivative-matching loss, where $\dot{V}^{\text{obs}}$ is estimated from observations by central finite differences and $\dot{\hat{V}}$ is the model RHS evaluated at the predicted state. This term encourages the learned dynamics, not just the integrated trajectory, to match the data;
  \item $\mathcal{L}_{\text{smooth}} = \sum_{g,j}\bigl(\hat{V}_g(t_{j+1}) - 2\hat{V}_g(t_j) + \hat{V}_g(t_{j-1})\bigr)^2$: a discrete second-difference smoothness penalty on the predicted trajectory, which discourages spurious high-frequency oscillations introduced by the neural sub-networks (this corresponds to the constant \texttt{$\lambda$\_SMOOTH} in the source code);
  \item $\mathcal{L}_{\text{negativity}} = \sum_{g,j}\max(0, -\hat{V}_g(t_{g,j}))^2$: penalizes non-physical negative volume predictions;
  \item $\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}} = \|\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{nn}}\|_2^2$: $L_2$ weight decay on neural-network parameters.
\end{itemize}
The two \emph{phase-dependent} weights $w_{\text{vol}}$ and $w_{\text{deriv}}$ implement curriculum-style scheduling: during the AdamW phase ($\text{phase}=1$) we use $w_{\text{vol}}=1.0$, $w_{\text{deriv}}=1.0$, prioritizing trajectory fitting; during the L-BFGS refinement phase ($\text{phase}=2$) we shift to $w_{\text{vol}}=0.8$, $w_{\text{deriv}}=1.5$, increasing the influence of the derivative-matching term on local refinement. The fixed weights are $\lambda_{\text{vol}}=1.0$, $\lambda_{\text{deriv}}=10^{-2}$, $\lambda_{\text{smooth}}=10^{-3}$, $\lambda_{\text{phys}}=0.1$, $\lambda_{\text{neg}}=10^{-3}$, $\lambda_{\text{reg}}=10^{-5}$, selected by grid search on training-loss convergence and biological consistency. The Phase~2 (structured-parameter UDE) loss replaces the smoothness and derivative terms with the consistency prior $\mathcal{L}_{\text{consistency}}$ defined in Eq.~\ref{eq:consistency_prior}, retaining $\mathcal{L}_{\text{volume}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{physics}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{negativity}}$, and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}$. The naming used here---\emph{smoothness}, \emph{consistency}, etc.---corresponds directly to the loss terms in the source code (\texttt{src/JuliaconSubmission.jl} and \texttt{src/FutureWorks.jl}); the \texttt{smooth\_penalty} variable in the code is the smoothness term above, and the \texttt{consistency\_loss} variable in the Phase~2 code is the consistency prior.

\subsection{Training Protocol}

Training uses a two-phase approach: (1) AdamW with learning rate $10^{-3}$, cosine decay, and up to 5\,000 iterations with validation-based early stopping, followed by (2) L-BFGS refinement from the best AdamW parameters (up to 500 iterations). Early stopping uses a patience of 200 iterations, meaning training halts if the validation loss does not improve for 200 consecutive iterations; the model checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is retained. L-BFGS terminates when the gradient norm falls below $10^{-8}$ (the default convergence criterion in Optim.jl) or the maximum iteration count is reached, whichever comes first.

\textbf{Note on AdamW iteration budget.} The 5\,000-iteration upper bound is intentionally loose: in practice, the validation-based early-stopping rule typically terminates the AdamW phase well before the cap is reached (the loss curves in Figures~\ref{fig:hybrid_training_loss}--\ref{fig:pure_training_loss} flatten by roughly $\sim$500--1\,000 iterations). Because the L-BFGS phase initializes from the lowest-validation-loss checkpoint, retaining the high cap incurs little additional cost and provides a safety margin for groups whose loss landscape has not yet stabilized; tightening the cap to $\sim$500--1\,000 iterations would yield comparable final fits at lower training cost. We prioritized robustness over efficiency given the relatively short per-fold training times ($\sim$15--30 minutes).

\textbf{Hyperparameter bounds.} The parameter ranges $r \in [0.01, 2.0]$~day$^{-1}$, $c \in [0.001, 10.0]$~day$^{-1}$, and $h \in [0.01, 5.0]$ (normalized volume units) were chosen based on published estimates from the tumor-modeling literature. Growth rates are bounded by experimentally observed doubling times for solid tumors \cite{vaghi2020gompertz}. Killing rate and half-saturation bounds span the physiologically plausible range reported in Kuznetsov et~al.\ \cite{kuznetsov1994} and de~Pillis et~al.\ \cite{depillis2005validated}, scaled to our normalized volume units. Sigmoid activations map unbounded neural network outputs into these intervals.

\subsection{Neural Network Architecture}

Both variants use custom residual blocks with layer normalization:
$\text{ResBlock}(\mathbf{x}) = \text{Activation}(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{W}_2 \sigma(\text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{W}_1 \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}_1)) + \mathbf{b}_2)$.
Phase~1 networks take 2 inputs $\rightarrow$ 32 hidden $\rightarrow$ 2 ResBlocks $\rightarrow$ 16 hidden $\rightarrow$ 1 output. Phase~2 networks take 3 inputs $\rightarrow$ 32 hidden $\rightarrow$ 2 ResBlocks $\rightarrow$ parameter-specific outputs. SELU activation is used for hidden layers \cite{teshima2020universal}.

\subsection{Computational Implementation}

We use Tsit5() with InterpolatingAdjoint() for gradient computation, with tolerances abstol $= 10^{-8}$, reltol $= 10^{-8}$. Training employs $N=3$ random initializations (seeds 1042, 2042, 3042) to mitigate local minima. Each initialization undergoes the full AdamW + L-BFGS training protocol, and the final model is selected as the one achieving the lowest total training loss (Eq.~\ref{eq:total_loss}) across all restarts.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Data}
\label{sec:data}

We use the publicly available murine tumor--immune dataset released by Atsou et~al.\ \cite{atsou2021size,atsou2022equilibrium} alongside their size- and space-structured tumor--immune interaction model. The data are distributed under a Creative Commons Zero (CC0) license at \url{https://github.com/kevin-atsou/tumorgrowth/tree/main/data} and originate from in~vivo experiments using the mSCC38 squamous cell carcinoma cell line in FVB/N wild-type mice, described in detail in \cite{atsou2022equilibrium} (Materials, Section~2.3). We obtained both CSVs unmodified from that repository and re-host them at \texttt{Data/} in our own repository for convenience.

The temporal dataset (\texttt{tumor\_time\_to\_event\_data.csv}) contains longitudinal tumor-volume measurements grouped by (KineticID, TumorID) pairs, where the KineticID values \{C2, C3, C4\} index three experimental immune-cell conditions and the TumorID values \{T1, \dots, T5\} index five tumor replicates / scenarios. For each pair the file records: observation time (days), tumor volume (mm\textsuperscript{3}), and the concurrently measured immune cell count. Measurements were taken at days $t \in \{14, 15, 21, 22, 28, 29, 36\}$, giving up to 7 time points per group (some groups have fewer due to missing or excluded measurements). A static dataset (\texttt{tumor\_volume\_vs\_Im\_cells\_rate.csv}) provides 39 paired measurements of tumor volume and immune killing rate (rank correlation $r = -0.82$), used for physics-informed constraints. The reported forecast windows in Section~\ref{sec:results} (e.g.\ days 15--36 in Figures~\ref{fig:c4t5_forecast}--\ref{fig:c4t5_forecasts_combined}) correspond to the empirical observation interval of this dataset; the lower bound is the first observation time and the upper bound is the last.

Tumor volumes are normalized by 1000~mm\textsuperscript{3} for numerical stability. Sparse immune measurements are interpolated using cubic splines. Data is grouped by \{KineticID, TumorID\}, excluding groups with fewer than 2 time points.

\subsection{Cross-Validation Design}

Each tumor group is identified by a pair \{KineticID, TumorID\}: the KineticID (C2, C3, C4) denotes the immune condition (e.g., concentration level of administered immune cells), and the TumorID (T1--T5) denotes the tumor replicate or scenario. The notation ``C2-T1'' thus refers to the trajectory measured under immune condition C2 for tumor scenario T1.

We employ a leave-one-group-out cross-validation scheme. For each held-out group (e.g., C2-T1), the training set comprises: (a)~data from all \emph{other} immune conditions for the same tumor type (e.g., C3-T1 and C4-T1), and (b)~data from the held-out condition for all \emph{other} tumor types (e.g., C2-T2, C2-T3, etc.). This design tests the model's ability to generalize across immune conditions for a given tumor type. Five groups are held out for evaluation: C2-T1, C2-T2, C2-T3, C2-T4, and C4-T5, selected to span different immune conditions and tumor scenarios.

\section{Software design}
\label{sec:software-design}

The implementation is a modular Julia package. \texttt{EnhancedPINODE.jl} implements the dual-network hybrid UDE (Phase~1). \texttt{PureMechanisticUDE.jl} implements the structured-parameter UDE (Phase~2). Neural networks use Flux.jl \cite{innes2018flux}, ODE integration uses DifferentialEquations.jl \cite{rackauckas2017de} with SciMLSensitivity.jl, and optimization uses Optimization.jl \cite{rackauckas2019diffeqflux}. The \texttt{test/} directory contains unit tests with CI via GitHub Actions. The package is available at \url{https://github.com/ans036/tumor-immune-ude} and requires Julia 1.9+.

\subsection{Parameter Count and the Nature of Model Complexity}
\label{sec:param-count}

A recurring motivation for hybrid modeling is that classical mechanistic models are ``parameter-heavy.'' We make this comparison explicit and, in doing so, clarify what it does and does not mean here. Table~\ref{tab:param_counts} reports exact counts; the UDE figures were extracted directly from the model objects (\texttt{length(Flux.destructure(nn)[1])}), and the classical figures from the reference model definitions.

The Kuznetsov--Taylor model is a two-compartment ODE system (effector cells $E$, tumor cells $T$) with eight kinetic parameters $\{s,p,g,m,d,a,b,n\}$ (BioModels \texttt{BIOMD0000000762}) \cite{kuznetsov1994}; the de Pillis--Radunskaya system adds NK-cell, CD8\textsuperscript{+}, and circulating-lymphocyte compartments together with therapy terms, on the order of twenty parameters \cite{depillis2005validated}. By contrast, our structured-parameter UDE (Phase~2) contains a single shared network of $5{,}476$ weights, and the dual-network hybrid (Phase~1) contains $10{,}242$ network weights plus three scalar parameters for each of the eleven groups it retains.

Read at face value, these counts show that the UDE has \emph{more} free parameters than the classical models, not fewer---as expected for a neural function approximator. The meaningful distinction is therefore not the raw count but the role each parameter plays. In a classical model, every parameter is a biologically named constant the modeler must fix or estimate, and Kuznetsov et~al.\ themselves note that several of their constants ``cannot be measured in~vivo'' and had to be inferred by fitting; with sparse data such constants are frequently non-identifiable. In the UDE, the neural weights are not individually interpreted---they are jointly regularized nuisance parameters---while the biologically meaningful quantities remain the same small set as the classical models ($r$, $K$, $c$, $h$), now recovered as data-driven, context-dependent functions rather than hand-tuned constants (Section~\ref{sec:immune-interp}).

The UDE thus does not reduce the raw parameter count; it reduces the \emph{modeler-specified structure} and the \emph{manual-calibration burden}. It requires no a~priori choice of the immune-interaction functional form, and its interpretable parameters are calibrated automatically under physics-informed regularization. We state this trade-off precisely here to avoid the earlier, looser suggestion of outright parameter parsimony.

\subsection{Phase 1: Hybrid UDE}

The hybrid UDE converges stably through both AdamW and L-BFGS phases, achieving $R^2 = 0.897$ (Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid_training_loss}). The Immune Response Network learns biologically meaningful patterns: volume-dependent activation consistent with antigen presentation, saturation at high immune fractions, and threshold effects at low immune levels (Figure~\ref{fig:irn_surface}). The Time-Aware Correction Network captures temporal effects not present in the mechanistic backbone (Figure~\ref{fig:tacn_surface}). While the correction magnitude does increase over time---reflecting growing discrepancy between the Gompertz backbone and observed dynamics---the \emph{rate of increase} in correction diminishes at late times, indicating that the learned correction approaches an asymptotic regime rather than diverging. We use ``stabilization'' to refer to this plateauing behavior of the correction's derivative, not a decrease in absolute magnitude. Learned parameters exhibit group-specific variation (Figure~\ref{fig:hybrid_params}): growth rates span $r \in [0.10, 0.34]$~day$^{-1}$ across groups, with the C4$|$T5 group learning the highest rate ($r = 0.34$~day$^{-1}$) consistent with its rapid observed growth. Immune strength parameters $\alpha$ range from 0.11 to 0.38, showing group-level variation that reflects the interplay between immune cell counts and tumor-specific sensitivity rather than a simple monotonic relationship.

\subsection{Interpretation of the Learned Immune Interaction}
\label{sec:immune-interp}

Section~\ref{sec:param-count} argued that the UDE's value lies in learning the immune-interaction \emph{form} rather than in parameter parsimony. Here we test whether the workflow actually recovers a mechanistically \emph{meaningful} immune term---the missing ingredient of plain Gompertz growth---rather than an opaque curve fit. We evaluate the trained Phase~2 killing rate $R_{\text{kill}}(V,I)=c(V,t,I)\,V\,I/(h(V,t,I)+V)$ over the observed data domain and characterise it quantitatively (Figure~\ref{fig:immune_interp}). Four properties emerge:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Monotone in immune level.} Holding volume at its median, $R_{\text{kill}}$ increases monotonically with immune-cell count across the observed range (Figure~\ref{fig:immune_interp}a), recovering the expectation that more effector cells produce more killing.
\item \textbf{Saturating in volume (Michaelis--Menten).} Holding immune level fixed, $R_{\text{kill}}$ rises and saturates with $V$, reaching half its maximum near $V\approx 256$~mm\textsuperscript{3}; the learned term thus reproduces the Michaelis--Menten shape as an emergent property of the fit rather than by fiat.
\item \textbf{Grounded in independent data.} The learned killing coefficient $c$ correlates with the held-out static killing-rate measurements at Spearman $\rho = 0.80$ (Figure~\ref{fig:immune_interp}b). Because these 39 measurements never enter the volume-fitting loss, the interaction is anchored to data beyond the trajectories it was trained on.
\item \textbf{Temporally structured.} For the representative group C4-T5, the killing \emph{efficiency} $c(t)$ peaks early ($\sim$day~22) whereas the realised killing \emph{rate} $R_{\text{kill}}(t)$ peaks later ($\sim$day~36), as the effector and tumor populations both accumulate (Figure~\ref{fig:immune_interp}e). This temporal ordering---efficiency leading realised effect---is a mechanistic insight that a fixed-parameter model cannot express.
\end{enumerate}

The learned parameters stay within biologically plausible ranges---$c \in [6.1, 9.4]$~day\textsuperscript{-1} and $h \in [0.9, 2.5]\times10^{3}$~mm\textsuperscript{3} (Figure~\ref{fig:immune_interp}d)---and vary smoothly with state rather than collapsing to constants, which is exactly the context-dependence the structured-parameter design targets. Decomposing $\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}t$ for C4-T5 (Figure~\ref{fig:immune_interp}c) shows Gompertz growth (up to $\sim$409~mm\textsuperscript{3}/day) and immune killing (up to $\sim$402~mm\textsuperscript{3}/day) largely cancelling to a small net rate ($\leq 28$~mm\textsuperscript{3}/day), so the immune term contributes a median of $87\%$ of the growth magnitude at observed points. We flag honestly that these two large, nearly-cancelling terms are a known tendency of over-expressive UDEs; the net trajectory is well-determined but the individual term magnitudes should be read with the interpretability caveats of Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. Taken together, the learned term is monotone in immune level, saturating in volume, grounded in independent measurements, and temporally structured---i.e.\ the workflow supplies the immune interaction that Gompertz growth lacks, which is the substantive advantage claimed over the classical baselines.

\section{Discussion}

The progressive workflow from hybrid to structured-parameter UDE demonstrates a practical research pattern: the hybrid model's flexibility reveals what dynamics matter (complex immune responses, temporal corrections), while the structured-parameter model channels these insights into an interpretable framework. The structured-parameter UDE's higher accuracy ($R^2 = 0.991$ vs.\ $0.897$) suggests that constraining neural networks to output biologically meaningful parameters acts as effective regularization.

The cross-validation results (mean $R^2 = 0.945$) indicate reasonable generalization, though we note the dataset is small (five held-out groups from a single experimental system). The gap between training $R^2$ (0.991) and cross-validation $R^2$ (0.945) suggests some overfitting, which is expected given the model's expressiveness relative to dataset size.

From a software perspective, Julia's SciML ecosystem provides a cohesive development experience. The composability of DifferentialEquations.jl, Flux.jl, and SciMLSensitivity.jl enables rapid prototyping of UDE architectures, and the performance characteristics (sub-millisecond inference, efficient adjoint gradients) are well-suited to iterative model development.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results.

\textbf{Limited baselines.} Our comparison includes only classical mechanistic ODE models (Gompertz, Kuznetsov--Taylor, de Pillis--Radunskaya). We do not compare against modern data-driven baselines such as Neural ODEs \cite{chen2018node}, latent ODE models, or Gaussian process regression, which could provide competitive or superior performance on this dataset. Future work should include these comparisons to properly contextualize the UDE approach.

\textbf{No ablation studies.} We do not systematically ablate model components (e.g., removing individual loss terms, varying network size, testing different mechanistic backbones). Such studies would clarify which design choices contribute most to performance and are an important direction for future work.

\textbf{No uncertainty quantification.} Our models produce point predictions without confidence intervals. For any downstream application, uncertainty estimates are essential. Techniques such as ensemble methods, Monte Carlo dropout, or Bayesian neural network extensions could provide this, but are not implemented here.

\textbf{Extrapolation beyond training range.} Because the Phase~2 parameter networks take normalized time $t$ as a direct input, learned parameter functions may not generalize well beyond the training temporal window. The sigmoid-bounded activations mitigate divergence, but long-term forecasts should be treated as extrapolations with increasing uncertainty.

\textbf{Naming and interpretability.} While we use the term ``structured-parameter'' to describe the Phase~2 architecture, it is important to note that neural networks learn all four biological parameters as functions of state. The model is not mechanistic in the traditional sense---it is a neural model whose outputs are structured through a mechanistic template. The biological interpretability of individual parameter trajectories should be treated with caution.

\textbf{Dataset scope.} The dataset comes from a single experimental system with a small number of tumor--immune trajectories. Generalization to other tumor types, species, or experimental conditions has not been demonstrated. The single-compartment model also neglects spatial heterogeneity and metastatic dynamics.

\textbf{Forecasting caveats.} The reported forecasting metrics are computed on this specific dataset and should not be interpreted as indicative of performance on independent datasets or in applied settings. Long-term forecasts beyond the training data's temporal extent are extrapolations and should be treated accordingly.

\section{Conclusions}

We have presented a progressive UDE workflow for tumor--immune dynamics modeling in Julia's SciML ecosystem. The dual-network hybrid UDE ($R^2 = 0.897$) serves as an exploratory tool, while the structured-parameter UDE ($R^2 = 0.991$, cross-validation mean $R^2 = 0.945$) demonstrates that channeling neural network outputs through classical equation structures can yield both high accuracy and parameter-level interpretability. The implementation showcases the capabilities of Julia's SciML ecosystem for composing differential equations with neural networks in a scientifically grounded manner. Important next steps include comparison against modern neural baselines, ablation studies, uncertainty quantification, and validation on larger and more diverse datasets.
